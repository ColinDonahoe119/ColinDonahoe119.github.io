This past weekend my roommates and I watched one of my favorite movies: The Social Network. One of the, pretty inconsequential, points of the movie got me especially inspired: when Zuckerberg goes to his blog to both rant about his ex-girlfriend and describe the creation of facemash. Not to say that movie's portrayal of Mark Zuckerberg is what I'm going after, I certainly will not be ranting about girls, and almost certainly not be documenting the creation of the next trillion dollar company. But I recognized a pattern of extremely smart and accomplished people sharing their insights via a blog, or a public diary. 

The best example of this I can name is Paul Graham, probably the greatest mind in startups. His blog on doing great work has genuinely been a blueprint for me, even if I haven't been able to apply it to the extent I have hoped for. It doesn't have to be one of the most influential thinkers either. A couple of weeks ago I stumbled across a linkedIn post which brought me to a blog from a Data Scientist in Philadelphia. His posts, which I thought were too high level at first, were really insightful once I got my mind around it. I actually had a conversation with him about Bayesian Inference, the topic of one of his posts, and I copied his github repo to host this blog. 

A lot of smart, successful people blog. So if I start a blog it will make me smarter and successful? Probably not. But, I don't think it's a coincidence. I think there is a lot of utility in blogs, and I'd like to expand on why. This is, admittingly, moreso an argument to myself. If I don't outline specifically why I am doing this, than unfortunately this idea will die a quick and forgettable death. I hope it doesn't though, and so here is why a blog is worth while:

1. It's a Journal
   Like I said earlier, I see blogging as a public journal, and journaling is good for you, especially in a distracted, consumed world. Writing, or typing, requires you to think and reflect. Reflection is intrinsically good, why else would monks do it for hours a day. It makes you look critically on yourself, and your ideas. Rather than doing things without thinking, or going through the motions because you're expected to, reflection makes you ask the questions which get at the foundation of you. Now, a blog is a little different in the sense it is not as personal as a diary, nor should it be. When I imagine my own posts, they will likely be about the world, or whatever I'm working on, rather than personal experience. But this reflection still holds that same value in a different way. If I'm making an argument or assertion, than I have to really examine the validity of the reasons for why I think that. Same if I'm presenting some coding project. I have to consider the applications, and ask "why does this matter?" or "is it meaningful?".

    Another thing about a journal: it's a time capsule of your thinking- what is most intrinsic to you. Time is a measure of change. When you don't change, time feels very fast. When you have no documentation of yourself, it can be difficult to see that change, and growth of thought. I hope to return to this in 10/20 years and see how much I've changed, and probably be embarrassed by how all over my thoughts can be. I hope this is that first height marker on the door frame at 3'6, and I get way taller.

2. Stimulates new and stronger ideas
   I feel I have the most fruitful thoughts when I'm pacing around and talking something out in my head. A blog will essentially be my pacing around notes. It's here where I strip concepts down, and try to convince myself of what I'm thinking about. Like I said earlier, this process of self examination is very important, and can really strengthen your reasoning, or make you realize you were completely wrong! Unfortunately, a lot of these pacing thoughts are fleeting. I might be ignited by some idea or fascinated by a topic, but then my brain asks what I should make for dinner and all of a sudden it's gone. If I convince myself to write about it though, it won't fly away with the wind. Rather, it will make me go far more in depth. In fact, until I finish the blog and feel happy with it, than I will have no choice but to continue thinking about it. It takes whatever good idea and locks it in a cage in my brain, and doesn't let it out until it's fleshed out enough to post.
   
3. Contribution, not Consumption
     These days, we consume, consume, consume. It's impossible to avoid. And consumption isn't inherently bad, but it needs to be weighed with contribution, especially with work. In fact, literally as writing this, I realized one of my biggest mental blockades in building Data Science projects: I don't know how to contribute. It's painfully obvious in hindsight. I want to become a Data Scientist, and to do that I need strong end-to-end projects. But as of now my entire motivation was to put a project on my resume. No wonder I have a hard time spending hours doing something with the end goal in mind to add couple of bullet points to a piece of paper.
   
   Over the summer, I did a remote internship with CBB Analytics, and was tasked with building an in-game win probability model. CBB Analytics is a startup with three employees. It was unpaid, I would meet once a week with the founder, and also did roughly 30-40 hours of lifeguarding on the side. Yet, it felt so easy to work on that project. Why? Because each step of the process I was thinking "Can't wait to show Nick what I did" and "this model will be in the site one day, used by coaching staffs". Oh, and I got to put it on my resume.

   The point is that contribution brings meaning to work, which brings quality to work. This is what differentiates a blog from a journal. Would I have spent this much time writing these arguments in a journal? Not a chance. But because there's the possibility, albeit slim, that someone reads this, I am putting in much more effort. Which leads me to my last and most important point
   
4. You can help people
   I said earlier that Paul Graham's blog on doing great work has been very beneficial for me. He'll never know it, but he made a real impact on someone. In fact, he probably made an impact on a couple thousand of people. But it only took one for the venture to be worth while. Maybe one of these posts can ignite an interest for someone. Maybe I can make someone think about something in a new or different way. Maybe they will go on to do great work, discover something great, or build a great company (In which case I'd like a cut). Not to be so naive, or cocky, to say that 21 year old me, who knows next to nothing about most things, is the one to inspire. But it's possible that what I'm thinking about resonates with someone else, and that is win. Who knows. Maybe I convinced YOU to start a blog. You probably don't exist. If you do, than I probably told you about this blog, or you are some internet straggler. Either way, whether you start a blog or not, I hope you thought in a constructive way.


So there you have it. I hope that I convinced myself that this is a worthwhile endeavor. You can tell if this is the first of many posts. I'm currently studying math, data science, and philosophy, so those are some topics you could expect from me. In particular, I've been thinking a lot about the debate of Ai and consciousness. We'll see if that makes it out of the drafts.
